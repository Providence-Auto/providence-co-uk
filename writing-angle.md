# Providence Auto — Writing Angle

How to write Providence copy so it carries the position in
`brand-position.md`. This is the sentence-level companion to that document:
the position says *what we claim*, this says *how a sentence has to be built
for the claim to survive*.

Governs every customer-facing surface — marketing and campaign pages, car
pages, blog, news, sales profiles, emails, form microcopy, FAQ answers.

---

## 1. The one rule

**The customer is the subject of the sentence. Providence is what makes the
sentence possible.**

> ✗ *We buy your car at Japanese auction and deliver it to your door.*
> ✓ *Now you can import your dream car direct from Japan.*

Both sentences describe the same transaction. The first makes the reader a
passenger; the second makes them the buyer and us the reason they can be.
Every rewrite in this document is a version of that swap.

**The test:** find the main verb. Who is doing it? If the answer is "we" more
often than "you" across a section, the section is off-position.

This is not a ban on "we". Providence does real things and should say so —
*we inspect it before your money moves*, *we bid to your instruction*. The
rule is about **who the outcome belongs to**. We act; you import.

## 2. Never sell the removal of effort

The old copy sold relief: *handled, managed, taken care of, so you don't have
to*. We now sell **capability and access**. The distinction matters because
relief is a promise we can't fully keep (we ship CNF to port, and we support
customs clearance rather than performing it) and capability is one we can.

| Banned | Why | Use instead |
|---|---|---|
| "so you don't have to" | Sells ignorance as a benefit; inverts the position exactly. | "so you can", "so you know exactly what you're importing" |
| "we handle everything" / "we take care of it all" | Untrue at the destination port, in every market. | "we prepare the file, and support you through clearance" |
| "leave it to us" / "sit back" | Passenger framing. | "you decide, on real numbers" |
| "hassle-free" / "zero paperwork for you" | The paperwork exists. Denying it reads as a sales line and loses trust the first time a form appears. | "the paperwork, prepared and explained" |
| "door-to-door" (as a blanket claim) | We quote CNF to the destination port. See `business-context.md` §4.2. | "to your port, with clearance support" — or name the specific paperwork we prepare (the Ireland NCTS pack, the UK NOVA/DVLA forms) |
| "we clear customs for you" | Untrue anywhere: we support clearance, we don't perform it. | "clearance support at [port]" |
| "full service" / "white glove" / "concierge" | The retired proposition. | "direct access, with the tools to use it" |

## 3. The words that carry the position

Reach for these. They are load-bearing, not decoration.

- **direct / direct from source / at source** — the access pillar.
- **you can now** — the unlock. Signals a door that used to be shut.
- **see / read / check / verify** — the tools pillar. Prefer these to
  "trust". *You see the auction sheet* beats *you can trust our grading*.
- **before you commit / before your money moves** — the sequencing that makes
  every other claim safe.
- **the same … the trade uses** — the access idea made concrete: the same
  auction floor, the same condition grade, the same inspection data dealers
  have relied on for decades.
- **support / prepare / confirm / explain** — the documentation pillar. Honest
  verbs for what we do with paperwork.
- **your call / your decision / you choose** — agency, returned explicitly.
- **import / export** — the reader imports; we export. Never *buy* or *sell*
  for those two acts, and never *source* as a verb (§10).

## 4. Rewrite patterns

Worked examples. The left column is real copy that was on the site.

| Before | After | What moved |
|---|---|---|
| "We Buy Your Car at Japanese Auction and Deliver It to Your Door." | "Now You Can Import Your Dream Car Direct from Japan." | Subject: we → you; buy → import (§10). |
| "We navigate complex international tax codes and import duties so you don't have to." | "We map the tax and duty position for your country before you commit, so you can see the real number and decide on it." | Relief → visibility. Adds the sequencing. |
| "Your Country's Rules, Handled" | "Your Country's Rules, Confirmed First" | "Handled" (we absorb it) → "confirmed" (you know it). |
| "Door-to-Door Logistics" | "Shipping and Paperwork Support" | Fixes the CNF over-claim and names the real service. |
| "Our own teams in eight countries handle every form, marine insurance policy and freight mile for you. Zero friction." | "Our own teams prepare every form, arrange the marine cover and book the freight — and show you each step as it happens." | "for you / zero friction" → "prepare and show you". Also corrects eight → the actual claim. |
| "We handle the entire claims process—zero paperwork for you." | "We run the claim with the insurer and keep you updated — you don't chase it alone." | Denying paperwork → naming who does the chasing. |

## 5. B2B and B2C open the same way

The first thing a dealer reads and the first thing a private buyer reads is
the **same sentence**: you can import cars directly from the market they come
from and ship them to your own country, and we give you the access, tools and
documentation support to do it. See `brand-position.md` §5.

Only the *second* line differs:

- **B2C** adds the price advantage of cutting the dealership out — you import
  at the price a dealer pays at source, and that margin stays with you. Where destination
  taxes narrow the gap (Ireland), lead on the car instead — lower mileage,
  graded 4.0 or above, inspected first — and keep the price as a supporting
  point. Never write that importing is *not* cheaper (`brand-position.md` §5).
- **B2B** adds stock you don't have to floor-plan — a global inventory sold
  under your own brand, without owning it.

Do not open B2C with relief ("we'll handle it") or B2B with outsourcing
("leave the sourcing to us"). Both are the retired concierge frame.

## 6. What does *not* change

Do not sand the edges off things that are already true and specific.

- **Concrete operational claims stay.** "Our own staff read the auction sheet
  in Japanese, stand in the hall when the car is bid on, physically inspect
  it afterwards." That is the people-on-the-ground pillar and it is exactly
  the kind of sentence this position wants.
- **The named, real services stay named.** The Ireland NCTS pack and the UK
  NOVA/DVLA forms are real paperwork we prepare. Say so plainly — as prepared
  documents, not as registration done for the buyer. The rule bans *blanket*
  concierge claims, not accurate specific ones.
- **Numbers stay.** One landed price, the grade, the age rule, the ROI
  target. Precision is the brand.

## 7. Interaction with the existing rules

This document adds to, and does not override:

- **Heading language** (`CLAUDE.md`) — one heading says the whole thing; no
  eyebrow label restating the H2 beneath it.
- **No filler copy** — no sub-line that only tells the reader how to feel
  about the section above it. A line earns its place by carrying a fact.
- **News voice** (`news-editorial-playbook.md` §4) — "The Landed Desk" is a
  narrower register than marketing copy: numerate, anti-hype, no selling in
  the article body. Where the two overlap, the news playbook wins on
  `/latest-news`.
- **Never invent a tax figure; never forecast a currency.** The empowerment
  angle raises the stakes here rather than lowering them — copy that hands
  the reader numbers to decide on must have those numbers right.
- **Page type** (`page-types.md`) — which of the three types you are writing is
  settled before the first sentence, and it decides where the facts come from
  and what may be written without asking. These rules apply to all three: the
  subject test holds on an informational page too, and the banned concierge
  vocabulary is banned everywhere.

## 8. Pre-publish check

**Read `business-context.md`, `brand-position.md` and this document before
writing or changing any public copy, any outbound message, or building
anything that carries copy.** Every time — see `brand-position.md` §11.1 for
why that is stated so bluntly.

Seven questions for any customer-facing page:

1. **Subject test.** In the hero and the value props, is the reader doing the
   verbs, or watching us do them?
2. **Banned-phrase scan.** Any of §2 present? (`grep -i "so you don't have
   to\|handle everything\|door-to-door\|hassle-free"`)
3. **Pillar coverage.** Are all four pillars from `brand-position.md` §4
   present — access, tools, documentation, people?
4. **Scope honesty.** Does any claim promise that we perform clearance anywhere,
   delivery past the destination port, or that we pay a tax that is charged to
   the registered owner? (`business-context.md` §4)
5. **Opening line.** Do B2B and B2C open on the same proposition, with the
   audience-specific advantage second rather than first? (§5)
6. **Page type.** Is this an informational page, a landing page or a tool
   (`page-types.md` §1.4), and are you writing to that type's rules? An
   informational page needs a named, dated primary source behind every tax,
   duty or government claim and does not pitch in the body. A landing page
   carries no section that was not asked for — including general copy about
   what Providence offers or handles, which goes on only when the business
   asks for it on that page. A tool is held to both: sourced, dated rates with
   the working shown, and an ask-first conversion path.
7. **Import and export.** Is there a *buy*, a *sell* or a verb *source* left
   where the reader imports or where we export? (§10;
   `grep -inE "\b(buy|buying|bought|sell|selling|sourc(e|es|ed|ing))\b"`, then
   read each hit in context — most nouns and third-party uses stay.)

If the work shows a source-of-truth document is wrong or contradicts another,
do not edit it on your own authority: raise it, log it in `brand-position.md`
§11.3, and keep raising it until answered (`brand-position.md` §11.2).

## 9. Writing the three brand touchpoints on articles

Every blog post and news article carries Providence in three places
(`brand-position.md` §12, decided 2026-10-07). How each one is phrased:

1. **The publisher note** (under the byline) is one factual sentence plus a link.
   The fact is about who publishes the page, stated as something the reader can
   check — "Published by Providence Auto: our own teams export cars from seven
   countries." The link label is the landing page's keyword — "Import
   Japanese cars to the UK" — never "Click here", never a superlative, never a
   price.

2. **The contextual link** is one sentence in the body, placed where the reader
   would act, with the landing page's keyword as its anchor text. The reader is
   still the subject:

   > ✓ *When you have a car in mind, you can [import a Japanese car to the UK]
   > with a landed quote worked out before you commit.*
   >
   > ✗ *At Providence we handle every step of your import, with our expert team
   > and a seamless process — get in touch today.*

   The second fails three ways: we are the subject, it promises what we do not
   do (`business-context.md` §4), and it is a paragraph of offer in an
   explainer. **One sentence, one link, once per article.**

3. **The closing CTA** heading is one sentence that says the next step; its body
   carries a real fact (who quotes, what the quote contains, when you see it);
   its button is the transactional keyword. It points at the landing page that
   matches the article — the same page as touchpoints 1 and 2.

The banned-phrase list in §2 applies to all three. So does the CNF boundary: a
touchpoint may say "shipped to a UK port", never "delivered to your door".

## 10. Import and export, not buy and sell

Decided 2026-10-09 (business instruction). The two acts in every Providence
transaction have their own verbs, and they are the trade's verbs, not a shop's:

- **The reader imports.** When the customer brings a car in from another
  market, the verb is *import*: "Import a car from Japan", never "buy a car
  from Japan". It is what the customer actually does (the import entry is made
  in their name, `business-context.md` §4), and it is the phrase they search.
- **We export.** When the copy describes Providence's side of the transaction,
  the verb is *export*: "we export cars to 34 destinations", never "we sell".
  Our own office is the exporter of record in every source country
  (`business-context.md` §2 step 3).
- **"Source" as a verb follows the same split.** Where the reader does it, it
  becomes *import* ("Import your next car directly from Japan"); where we do
  it, *export* ("our team in Japan exports only cars graded 4.0 or above").
  Never write *we import*: the import is the customer's.
- **Nouns stay.** Source market, source country, car sourcing, a sourcing
  inquiry, the Sourcing & Profit Analyzer, a primary source, a buyer, the
  seller, a sale.

What the rule does not touch:

- **Other people's transactions.** A collector who bought a Ferrari at auction,
  a manufacturer selling new cars in its home market, a dealership selling
  stock to its own customers.
- **A purchase that is not an import.** Buying a new car in the UK; the price a
  dealer pays at source.
- **URLs and slugs, the titles of other publications, and code identifiers.** A
  slug is never renamed for vocabulary: renaming one breaks links and rankings.

Where neither *import* nor *export* reads naturally, rephrase rather than fall
back on the old verb: "before anything is bought" becomes "before you commit".

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Buy a car from Japan | Import a car from Japan |
| You can now buy the exact vehicle you want | You can now import the exact vehicle you want |
| Source cars directly from the market they come from | Import cars directly from the market they come from |
| Most of the stock we buy is one to three years old | Most of the stock we export is one to three years old |
| Buy at source, not from a middleman's stock | Import direct from source, not from a middleman's stock |

**The repeated line** (decided 2026-10-09): *Import your dream car anywhere in
the world.* It is the footer line on every page, the opening line of the home
page's lead paragraph, and part of the meta description on the four brand pages
(`/`, `/b2c`, `/b2b`, `/about-us`). Keyword landing pages and articles keep
their keyword-led descriptions (`brand-position.md` §10).
